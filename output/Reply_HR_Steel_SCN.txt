SECTION 4: Ground B – Invocation of Section 74 is Wholly Unsustainable; No Fraud, Wilful Misstatement or Suppression
-----------------------------------------------------------------
<p>The invocation of the extended period and Section 74(1) of the CGST Act, 2017 is patently misconceived and legally unsustainable. Section 74 can be invoked only where there exists (i) fraud, (ii) wilful misstatement, or (iii) suppression of facts, and each of these ingredients must be with an intent to evade tax. The burden to establish these ingredients lies squarely upon the Revenue and cannot be discharged by mere assertion or by a pro forma recital in the SCN.</p><p>The Hon'ble Supreme Court in <em>Pushpam Pharmaceuticals Company v. CCE, Bombay [1995 (78) ELT 401 (SC)]</em> has authoritatively held that "suppression" must be deliberate and with an intent to escape payment of duty; mere omission or non-payment is not enough. This position has been consistently followed in <em>CCE v. Chemphar Drugs & Liniments [1989 (40) ELT 276 (SC)]</em>, <em>Cosmic Dye Chemical v. CCE [1995 (75) ELT 721 (SC)]</em>, <em>Uniworth Textiles Ltd. v. CCE [2013 (288) ELT 161 (SC)]</em>, and more recently in <em>CCE v. Northern Operating Systems Pvt. Ltd. [2022 (61) GSTL 129 (SC)]</em>.</p><p>In the present case: (i) the Noticee duly reflected each and every invoice in its GSTR-3B returns, which are matched with the supplier's GSTR-1 auto-populating in GSTR-2A/2B — hence there is no "non-declaration" and no "suppression" within the meaning of Explanation 2 to Section 74; (ii) the Noticee cooperated with the investigation, appeared for statements on 05.07.2022, 21.12.2022 and 30.03.2026, and submitted such records as were available (the balance being in departmental custody); (iii) no positive act of concealment, no fabricated document, and no collusion has been alleged or proved against the Noticee; (iv) allegations pertaining to vehicle numbers, conduct of transporters, and dealings of the suppliers with their upstream suppliers (M/s Gajanan Enterprise, M/s Gauri Ispat, M/s Tej Steel) are matters entirely beyond the knowledge and control of the Noticee — the Noticee, being a downstream purchaser, cannot be visited with the consequences of alleged upstream misdemeanours. Reference is made to CBIC Instruction No. 01/2022-23 [GST-Investigation] dated 25.05.2022 and Circular No. 171/03/2022-GST dated 06.07.2022, wherein the Board itself recognises that penal action for fake ITC must be preceded by clear evidence of connivance.</p><p>Accordingly, at the highest, only Section 73 could have been invoked, and the SCN having been issued under Section 74 without any of its jurisdictional ingredients being satisfied, the entire proceeding is without jurisdiction and liable to be dropped in limine.</p>

SECTION 5: Ground C – Third-Party Statements, Discrepancies in Vehicle Numbers and Non-Examination of Transporters Cannot Fasten Liability on the Noticee
-----------------------------------------------------------------
<p>The entire edifice of the impugned SCN rests on: (a) statements of the proprietors of the two supplier firms; (b) statements/letters of some 23 transporters (out of hundreds of transactions); and (c) alleged discrepancies in vehicle registration numbers noted from the E-Vahan portal. Each of these grounds is legally infirm.</p><p><strong>(a) Third-party statements are not admissible without cross-examination.</strong> The statements of Shri Hafizullah Ajimullah Choudhari (RUD-1) and Shri Ajimullah Naim Choudhari (RUD-2) are self-serving statements by co-accused seeking to shift blame; such statements have no evidentiary value unless (i) the makers are examined-in-chief before the adjudicating authority, and (ii) the Noticee is granted an opportunity to cross-examine. This is the ratio of <em>Andaman Timber Industries v. CCE [(2015) 15 SCC 785]</em>, followed uniformly by all High Courts. Denial of cross-examination amounts to serious violation of natural justice and vitiates the proceedings.</p><p><strong>(b) Vehicle number discrepancies are, at worst, discrepancies in the supplier's e-way bill entries.</strong> Under the GST scheme, e-way bills are generated by the consignor/transporter. The Noticee, as the recipient, has no role in generating the e-way bill or entering vehicle numbers. The proprietor of the Noticee has categorically stated (in RUD-6) that he had never physically verified vehicle numbers, and that in a "free delivery" arrangement, such verification is neither expected of, nor practicable for, the recipient. Adverse inference on account of clerical errors in the supplier's e-way bill data — including transposition of digits or reuse of numbers — cannot be drawn against the Noticee absent evidence of connivance.</p><p><strong>(c) Sample-based statements of 23 transporters cannot be extrapolated.</strong> The impugned SCN itself admits at Para 3.8 that transporter statements were recorded "on sample basis." Of these 23, several (Sr. Nos. 8, 10, 11, 12, 13, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21) admit to having transported goods to various entities, and two (Sr. Nos. 14, 15) could not confirm with certainty. Only a fraction categorically denied transportation. To leap from such a small and inconclusive sample to a sweeping conclusion that the entirety of ITC of ₹11,36,93,005/- is fake is a leap not warranted by the evidence. Quantum-wise correlation between the denying transporters and specific invoices has not been demonstrated in the SCN.</p><p><strong>(d) The SCN admits inability to quantify.</strong> At Para 3.12, the department itself candidly records that "it is not feasible to segregate the value of ITC attributable to actual receipt of goods from that pertaining to supplies without goods" and "it is also not possible to quantify the exact amount of ITC passed on without supply of goods." A demand which the issuing authority itself concedes to be incapable of precise quantification is legally unsustainable. A demand must be based on evidence, not on estimation or presumption — see <em>Oudh Sugar Mills v. UOI [1978 (2) ELT J172 (SC)]</em>.</p>
